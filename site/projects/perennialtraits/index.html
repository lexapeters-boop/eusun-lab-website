PerennialTraits

Showcasing de novo domestication of perennial grain crops for profitable, resilient and sustainable agriculture

We are developing novel perennial crops that produce grain for people year after year, without re-sowing. Their deep root systems make farming more sustainable and more resilient to climate change. We identify perennial candidates with high grain yield, test how well their shoots and roots regenerate, and evaluate their potential to store carbon in the soil, using digital sensing, machine learning, X-ray imaging and isotope analysis.

Why perennial grains?

Annual crops must be sown every year. Tillage causes erosion, and their shallow roots miss nutrients and water deeper in the soil, which leads to nitrate leaching and water pollution. Perennial grain crops stay in the field for several seasons and keep living roots in the soil all year. Their deep roots capture more resources, feed soil microbes and build soil carbon. The challenge is that perennial grain yields are still low (about 20% of annual wheat for intermediate wheatgrass), and breeding is slow because key traits only show late in a plant's life.

Our goal

To speed up domestication of new perennial grain crops by finding the traits that matter for profit (grain and biomass), resilience (deep roots) and sustainability (soil health). We look for candidates that show:

  1. 01Better grain yield and survivalpredicted from early-life traits
  2. 02Faster shoot and root recoverywhen cut for forage or biomass (dual-purpose use)
  3. 03Greater root carbon input to the soil(rhizodeposition), also under drought

What we do

WP1

Grain and shoot traits: finding promising plants early.

We grow about 1,000 individual plants each of intermediate wheatgrass, silphium and sainfoin in the field. Drones carry hyperspectral, RGB, LiDAR and thermal sensors to measure seed vigour, canopy height, leaf chemistry and photosynthesis. Machine learning links these early signals to later grain yield, so that the best candidates can be selected without waiting for years (phenomic selection).

WP2

Shoot and root traits: how well do plants regrow?

Single plants grow in tubes and are scanned with X-ray CT every few days after cutting, to see when and how roots resume growth. In the field, minirhizotron tubes and a multispectral camera follow root growth down to 2 m depth, with rain-out shelters to create drought. Root images are analysed with RootPainter, a deep-learning tool we co-developed.

WP3

Root-soil traits: how much carbon do roots put into the soil?

We label plants with carbon isotopes (¹¹C and ¹³C) to follow how much carbon moves from the shoot to the roots and soil, and how much is stabilised in different soil carbon pools. Nitrogen and hydrogen isotopes (¹⁵N, ²H) show how deeply plants take up nitrogen and water, using our core-labelling technique.

Expected outcomes

  • A tested way to select perennial candidates early with sensors and machine learning
  • Candidates that regrow quickly after cutting and keep their roots healthy
  • Evidence for how perennial roots improve soil health and store carbon, also under drought

Funding

PerennialTraits is funded by the Independent Research Fund Denmark (DFF) through a Sapere Aude: DFF-Starting Grant (Research Leader), 2025–2028, of about 828,000 EUR. The grant funds a PhD student and a postdoc in the Department of Agroecology at Aarhus University, together with the field, imaging and isotope work described above.

What does "Sapere Aude" mean?

Sapere aude is Latin for "dare to know" (often read as "dare to be wise"). DFF uses it as the name of its starting-grant programme for talented early-career researchers who want to build their own research group and take bold, original ideas forward.
